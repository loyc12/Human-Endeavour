# Phonetic Mapping and Semantic Translation

**Type: Setting reference**

**Status: Current foundation**

**Setting role: Signature implementation**

## Intended Use

This document owns the general communication-device concept. Species-specific language and sensory requirements belong in their respective references once established.

## Current Foundation

Translation systems allow speakers of different languages and species to communicate, but do not make communication frictionless. They can provide semantic translation and, where useful, phonetic mapping: an assisted attempt to render one speaker's sounds through another language's phonological or equivalent system.

Semantic translation is by far the easiest way to communicate with an individual whose language you do not or cannot speak yourself, but creates conceptual barriers and introduces a translation delay, which often makes communication between two species annoying and tedious.

Phonetic mapping can let a speaker practise a second language, preserve some of their audible effort and identity, or make the relationship between original speech and translation more visible. Users do not learn to speak and understand the base version of the target language, but a phonetically tweaked version, which translates phonemes into species-compatible ones on the fly. It may be slower, more cognitively demanding, and less fluent than semantic translation, but allows near-instantaneous speech, and much deeper cultural and conceptual shared understanding, and circumvents the need to learn to recognize and produce species-specific phonemic inventories or equivalent systems.

## Constraints and Consequences

- A translated meaning is not necessarily a full cultural equivalent. Idiom, register, implication, and unfamiliar concepts may need explanation.
- Species with different senses or vocal apparatuses may require forms of translation that are not purely auditory.
- People can reasonably prefer different degrees of assistance, from direct semantic translation to phonetic practice or untranslated speech.
- Delays, errors, privacy, consent, accessibility, and the ownership of translation data are social as well as technical concerns.
- Xenolinguistic expertise remains valuable because translation systems require calibration, contextual knowledge, and the detection of false equivalences.

## Open Design Questions

- Can a device translate thought, vocal speech, signed languages, chemical signals, or only particular inputs?
- Which parts of a translation are local to the user, shared with a room, or sent through a network?
- Can users inspect or correct a translation, and how is this handled during diplomacy, contracts, or testimony?
- What kinds of cognitive fatigue or sensory overload does sustained assistance create?
