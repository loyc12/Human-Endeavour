# Technological Horizons and Contact History

**Status: Approved setting baseline; specific societies open**

## Purpose

This document distinguishes the first fully contacted independently spacefaring near-peer from the wider field of independent life and society. It establishes only the contact horizon needed to constrain Milky Way demographics, ethics, and political memory; it does not catalogue species or frontier worlds.

## Selected Contact Horizon

### The singular near-peer encounter

The civilisation described in [[02 World/01 History/02 Independent Technological Civilisations|Independent Technological Civilisations]] is the first, and so far only, independently spacefaring civilisation met in sustained reciprocal contact. At contact about 190 BCD it already possessed mature in-system settlement, independent science, and a few slow extrasolar footholds. Human FTL networks created a major mobility and industrial asymmetry, but did not create the civilisation's capacity for diplomacy, science, or political choice.

This encounter is culturally singular within ordinary human space. It decisively disproves any complacent belief that all spacefaring people are human descendants, human creations, or the products of human expansion. Its diaspora, its own continuing polities, and its participation in shared institutions make that fact part of ordinary Core life rather than a remote scientific curiosity.

### Other independent societies

Independent non-spacefaring societies are rare but become more frequently encountered as FTL-era exploration reaches a much larger and more varied frontier than the old human diaspora. They may include mobile foraging societies, urban or state societies using durable low-energy technologies, worlds whose institutions or ecology impede industrial takeoff, and societies recovering from earlier collapse. These descriptions identify historical and material conditions, not permanent civilisational ranks or biological destinies.

Such societies are not automatically candidates for intervention, technological transfer, uplift, or political incorporation. Their ability to understand contact, make collective decisions, refuse access, and determine their own pace is a central ethical and political question. The detailed law and practice remain open.

### Recent remote evidence

In the last few decades, remote frontier surveys have produced evidence or very limited contact suggesting that further independent technological societies may exist. These cases are distant, poorly understood, and not yet connected by sustained migration, diplomacy, or common institutions. They do not displace the gas-giant-moon civilisation as the only fully contacted independently spacefaring near-peer in the cultural experience of the Core.

## Consequences

- The first near-peer encounter becomes a historical shock, a major subject of xenology, and a continuing source of institutional reform rather than merely another species profile.
- The existence of other, more remote candidate societies prevents this shock from becoming a permanent claim that the near-peer is cosmically unique.
- Frontier contact policy must contend with unequal knowledge, quarantine, ecological disruption, trade pressure, and the risk that apparently benevolent assistance becomes coercive dependency.
- The crew can plausibly treat one independent near-peer's presence as ordinary while treating remote frontier reports and non-spacefaring contact as unfamiliar, contested, and politically unfinished.

## Boundaries for Later Work

- Do not assign a technological level, culture, moral outlook, or destiny to a species as a whole.
- Do not assume every detected society becomes part of human space or wishes to become interstellar.
- Develop individual frontier contacts only when needed for a narrative or political question, beginning with their material conditions, internal disagreement, and agency.
- Keep the number, locations, and exact status of recent remote technological detections open until frontier geography and FTL navigation are developed.
