# Settlement Topology and Connectivity

**Status: Approved setting baseline**

## Scope

This document owns the selected answer to point 3 of [[99 Workshop/05 Milky Way Astropolitical Foundations|Milky Way Astropolitical Foundations]]: how human-touched space is spatially continuous, where practical connections exist, and why mapped space is not the same as integrated space. It builds on the settlement genealogy in [[04 Pre-FTL Diaspora Geography|Pre-FTL Diaspora Geography]] without fixing a particular FTL mechanism, navigation limit, or complete star map.

## Selected Topology

Human-touched space is a **nested archipelago of civilisational clusters and high-value corridors**. It resembles a root or fungal web more than a set of concentric rings: frequently useful paths attract traffic, support, settlement, and further investment, which in turn make those same paths more useful. The historical core remains materially important because it contains the densest, oldest, and most redundant portion of the web, not because it directly controls every branch.

The pattern recurs at several scales. Sol and its immediate neighbours form the most mutually connected historical region. Major old worlds and civilisation-complexes possess their own locally dense bubbles. Corridors between such centres contain intermediate systems, stations, habitats, and service communities; locations near a practical midpoint or junction can become disproportionately important. The farthest settlement is more often a thin chain, a branch endpoint, or an isolated node than the edge of a smooth frontier.

“Midway” is a network role rather than an exact geometric point. A system becomes a midway settlement because it divides a difficult journey, connects several onward routes, provides useful resources or repair capacity, or becomes a natural site for transfer, waiting, services, and social mixing. Such places can grow from minor outposts into major corridor worlds even when they were initially less attractive than their endpoints.

## Connectivity Vocabulary

The following categories distinguish knowledge, physical access, settlement, and integration. They can describe a system, habitat, route, or region; a place can advance through them over time.

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Mapped** | Known through survey, probes, archives, observation, or reported contact. No current human presence is implied. |
| **Reachable** | A journey can be arranged in principle, but may be exceptional, expensive, slow, or unsupported. Regular traffic and a local community are not implied. |
| **Settled / reached** | A physical presence has taken root: a resident community, permanent station, or recurrent staffed outpost. It can still be small, dependent, and weakly connected. |
| **Integrated** | Maintained access, recurring movement, practical institutional links, and meaningful social or economic exchange make the place part of a wider regional public life. |

Settlement density is a separate modifier. A **lightly settled** system has a minimal or specialised human presence—because it is new, distant, relatively undesirable, locally difficult, strategically narrow in purpose, or simply not yet worth large investment. A **heavily settled** system supports a large and diversified population, multiple substantial habitats or communities, local industry and services, and several meaningful outward connections. Neither term implies that a place is permanently destined to remain sparse or dense.

## Connectivity as a Graph

Practical connectedness is best measured by the topology of usable routes rather than by straight-line distance or the fraction of nearby stars inhabited.

| Topological character | Practical condition |
| --- | --- |
| **Isolated node** | No dependable onward connection, or only rare episodic access. |
| **One-dimensional branch** | A system lies on a chain or at its end, with one consequential onward route. Disruption, delay, or neglect of that route has outsized effects. |
| **Two-dimensional mesh** | Several routes and loops connect local centres. People and goods can take alternatives, and no single link defines ordinary regional life. |
| **Three-dimensional web** | Multiple independent routes lead through several directions and scales. This is a measure of route redundancy and choice, not literal geometry. |

The true core is the most three-dimensionally connected part of human space. Major old-world regions commonly contain two-dimensional meshes surrounded by one-dimensional branches. The reconnection belt is mixed: important corridors and local meshes coexist with sparse branches and lightly settled nodes. The outer frontier is chiefly one-dimensional or isolated.

## Astrography, Interest, and Gaps

Gaps persist chiefly because settlement follows interest, not because empty space is inherently impassable. Local **astrography**—stellar density and type, accessible orbital resources, environmental prospects, and the distribution of attractive destinations—makes some regions richer in viable or desirable next steps than others. Migration demand, founding capital, inherited settlement paths, local priorities, and competing uses of labour then reinforce those differences.

This does not make sparse regions permanently excluded. Given enough time, a reachable system may reasonably be expected to be reached, settled, and developed. The present nevertheless contains lightly settled systems and enduring gaps because the relevant timescale is long, available effort is finite, and a nearer or more desirable project repeatedly wins the current decision. The model deliberately does not attribute those gaps to a specific FTL range, navigation hazard, or technical prohibition; those questions remain open.

## Reconnection and Corridor Growth

FTL reconnection strengthens the existing web without instantly regularising it. Early routes preferentially join places that already possess population, technical capacity, archives, commercial demand, or strategic significance. High-use connections then accumulate services, migration, standards, capital, and social familiarity, producing a self-reinforcing road structure. Conventional [[01 Technologies/01 Key Concepts/01 System-Scale Beam Arrays|beam-array networks]] persist alongside FTL corridors for intra-system logistics and slow, uncrewed interstellar resource flows, rather than being superseded by them.

The resulting network is core-weighted but not exclusively Sol-centred. Historical Sol-to-foundation paths and links among major legacy centres form important trunks; later direct regional links create denser meshes within mature space. The sparse frontier remains branch-like and dependent on comparatively few connections.

Directed corridor projects can cut across this organic pattern when an endpoint is unusually valuable. They are rare because they consume exceptional political attention, capital, and coordination. The direct connection between the true core and the independently spacefaring near-peer's contact region is one such project: first contact occurs through an outer-diaspora expedition, while later core-to-contact access becomes a deliberately prioritised link rather than merely the next increment of ordinary network growth.

## Consequences for Later Work

- Corridor communities, midway systems, junction habitats, and branch endpoints are natural sites of exchange, mixed populations, dependency, and distinctive local politics.
- A system's route topology may matter more to its lived wealth, safety, political leverage, and cultural orientation than its radial distance from Sol.
- Exact route capacity, speed, communications latency, and the infrastructure needed for high-speed service remain dependent on [[99 Workshop/02 Spacetime Engineering|Spacetime Engineering]].
- The next questions should determine how FTL changes the distinction between reachable and integrated, then how this network shapes reconnection, markets, governance, and population distribution.
