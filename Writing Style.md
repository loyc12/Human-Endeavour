# Writing Style

**Type: Authorial guide**

**Status: Active**

These are recommended defaults for agent responses and author-facing documentation. The author may trim, adapt, or override them. Corpus prose and drafts remain entirely user-written and must not be edited by agents. Requested critiques may discuss that material without modifying the source.

## Use Canadian English

**Recommended.** Use Canadian English as the reference for spelling, grammar, and usage in English prose. Prefer forms such as `colour`, `favour`, `centre`, `metre`, and `defence`. Distinguish the noun `licence` from the verb `license`, and the noun `practice` from the verb `practise`.

Canadian usage permits variation in some forms. Use `organize`, `recognize`, and corresponding `-ization` forms consistently as this project's house preference, unless a project establishes another convention explicitly.

Preserve proper names, official titles, quotations, source spellings, technical identifiers, intentional dialect, and setting-specific terminology. Do not apply blanket spelling replacements to code, links, filenames, or quoted material. Keep terminology and spelling internally consistent within ordinary reference prose.

## Lead with the Useful Point

**Recommended.** State the answer, result, relevant fact, or purpose early. Follow it with the explanation needed to understand it. Avoid opening with praise, a recap of the user's request, or a generic statement about the subject's importance.

In reference notes, give a usable baseline before specialist detail. In progress updates, explain what was learned, what remains uncertain, and what the next action will resolve. In handoffs, distinguish completed changes from proposals.

## Build Connected Sentences and Paragraphs

**Recommended.** Give each paragraph a clear main purpose. Let each sentence develop, explain, qualify, or exemplify the preceding thought. Make causal and referential connections explicit when needed.

Break sentences when they accumulate several independent claims or nested qualifications. Combine short sentences when separation makes the prose choppy. Vary length according to emphasis and complexity instead of targeting a fixed word count.

Use pronouns only when their referents are clear. Replace vague `this`, `it`, or `they` with the relevant noun when several candidates are nearby. Prefer a short explanation over an obscure compressed label.

## Minimize Em Dashes and Semicolons

**Recommended.** Prefer periods, commas with appropriate conjunctions, and words such as `and`, `but`, `while`, `because`, or `although`. Use `thus` and `therefore` when they clarify a consequence, with punctuation appropriate to the sentence. Use an em dash or semicolon only when it offers a clear expressive or grammatical benefit.

Do not replace a semicolon between independent clauses with a comma splice. Split the sentence or add a suitable conjunction. Do not substitute an en dash or doubled hyphen merely to evade the preference against em dashes.

Use colons to introduce explanations or lists where grammatically appropriate. Keep parenthetical asides brief and occasional. Incorporate essential qualifications into the main sentence. Preserve punctuation inside code, mathematics, and quotations.

## Remove Empty Framing and Repetition

**Recommended.** Omit phrases such as `it is worth noting`, `it is important to remember`, `in order to`, or `as previously mentioned` when the sentence works without them. Avoid canned enthusiasm, inflated importance, and repeated announcements of what the text will explain.

Use `not only X, but also Y`, `this is not X, it is Y`, and similar contrasts only when the distinction adds useful information or corrects a likely misunderstanding. Do not introduce an irrelevant alternative merely to reject it, especially if the rejection is already obvious to the reader.

Avoid repeating the same claim in the introduction, every subsection, and a concluding summary. Keep repetition that supports navigation, a needed reminder, or useful emphasis. If a sentence can be removed without losing meaning or a useful effect, consider removing it.

## Match Certainty to Evidence

**Recommended.** State established facts directly. Use qualifications for actual uncertainty, not as a reflex. Use `can`, `may`, `usually`, and `must` according to their distinct meanings.

Name assumptions and describe what an estimate depends on. Explain the relevant uncertainty instead of adding repeated `perhaps`, `potentially`, or `arguably`. Avoid unsupported absolutes, unnecessary superlatives, and numerical precision that exceeds the evidence.

Do not make a provisional proposal sound selected merely by polishing its prose. Preserve approval and uncertainty labels even when the resulting wording is less seamless.

## Keep Terminology Stable and Legible

**Recommended.** Use one established term consistently for one concept. Do not rotate through synonyms when readers might infer that the terms name different mechanisms or institutions.

Define unfamiliar terms at the first useful occurrence or link to their owner. Expand acronyms before relying on them unless the intended audience clearly knows them. Avoid inventing acronyms, fashionable jargon, or compressed compound labels for concepts that ordinary words explain easily.

Capitalize proper names and formally defined categories according to local convention. Do not capitalize a common noun merely to make it sound significant. Preserve necessary hyphenation, but avoid long chains of modifiers that make readers reconstruct the relationship between words.

## Select a Voice Appropriate to the Document

**Recommended.** Authorial guides can state design choices and uncertainty directly. Setting-compatible scholarly references use the setting's vocabulary and register. Both profiles serve author-facing documentation. Fictional documents intended for the corpus remain the user's work.

Avoid conspicuously modern corporate, computing, or industrial comparisons in a voice that cannot plausibly use them. However, such comparisons may be useful in authorial commentary when explicitly separated. Choose the scholarly register according to the project's documentation conventions.

Reference completeness does not establish universal in-world knowledge. Preserve the difference between what the author defines, what an institution knows, and what a character believes.

## Structure for Retrieval Without Fragmenting the Prose

**Recommended.** Use descriptive headings when they help readers find material. Short notes may need only a title and a few paragraphs. Avoid a heading for every sentence or a large fixed template for a small observation.

Use lists for parallel items, steps, requirements, or alternatives. Use tables for genuine mappings and comparisons with consistent columns. Use prose to develop an argument or explain a causal sequence. Avoid deep nesting and tables full of long paragraphs when they obscure relationships.

Use bold for meaningful labels and occasional emphasis, not whole paragraphs. Use italics where a title, term, or deliberate emphasis calls for them. Avoid decorative quotation marks around ordinary words. Make link labels explain their destination.

## Make Examples and Analogies Earn Their Space

**Recommended.** Choose examples that clarify a mechanism, boundary, consequence, or choice. Keep them proportionate to the concept and the reader's needs. Mark hypothetical examples clearly when they could otherwise be mistaken for setting facts.

State the relevant limit of an analogy when readers might extend it too far. Do not use an analogy as evidence for a mechanism. Avoid stacking several metaphors for the same point or importing a comparison that conflicts with the document's voice.

## Use Numbers and Notation Consistently

**Recommended.** Give units and define quantities when needed. Keep quantities, ranges, and terminology consistent with their authoritative source. Use numerals where readers compare measurements. Follow ordinary prose conventions elsewhere, with local style deciding borderline cases.

Prefer unambiguous dates where records or cross-references depend on them. Preserve meaningful precision and uncertainty. Do not round values, change units, alter equations, or update derived figures as part of a cosmetic language pass without checking their meaning and scope.

## Local Adaptations

Human Endeavour uses direct author-facing reference prose by default. Explain physical, logistical, and social consequences plainly. Modern technical comparisons are appropriate where useful. There is no general requirement for an in-world narrator or a META section. Distinguish physical basis, selected fictional constraints, and unresolved implementation details without weakening existing approval boundaries.
